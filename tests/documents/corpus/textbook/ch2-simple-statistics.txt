Chapter 2: Simple Statistics
============================

This chapter shows how to describe a set of numbers with a few well chosen summaries. It
covers the centre of a data set, its spread and its shape, how to spot values that do not
fit, and how to measure whether two quantities move together. The examples use small data
sets so that every step can be checked by hand.

Throughout the chapter a running example is used: the flow rate of twelve pumps of the
same model, measured on a test bench in litres per minute. The readings, sorted from
lowest to highest, are 36, 37, 38, 38, 39, 39, 39, 40, 40, 41, 42 and 47.

2.1 Data and variables

A data set is a collection of observations, and each observation records one or more
variables. A variable is any quantity or quality that can differ from one observation to
the next, such as the flow rate of a pump, the colour of a car or the number of faults
found in a week.

Variables are numerical when they are counts or measurements, and categorical when they
place an observation in a group. Numerical variables are discrete when they can only take
separate values, like a count of faults, and continuous when they can take any value in a
range, like a temperature.

The kind of variable decides which summaries make sense. An average colour means nothing,
but the most common colour does. Most of this chapter deals with numerical variables.

2.2 Measures of centre

A measure of centre is a single number that stands for a typical value of the data. There
are three in common use, and each has its strengths.

2.2.1 The mean

The mean is the sum of the values divided by how many there are. It is what most people
call the average. For the pump readings the sum is 476 and there are twelve readings, so
the mean is 476 / 12, about 39.7 litres per minute.

The mean uses every value, which makes it efficient, but it is pulled towards extreme
values. The single high reading of 47 raises the mean of the pump data by more than half
a litre per minute.

2.2.2 The median

The median is the middle value when the data are sorted. With an odd number of values it
is the one in the middle. With an even number it is the mean of the two middle values.

The pump data have twelve values, so the median is the mean of the sixth and seventh
values, which are both 39. The median is therefore 39 litres per minute.

The median is not affected by how extreme the largest or smallest values are. If the
reading of 47 were 70 instead, the median would still be 39 while the mean would rise to
about 41.6. For this reason the median is preferred for data with a long tail, such as
house prices or repair times.

2.2.3 The mode

The mode is the value that occurs most often. In the pump data the value 39 occurs three
times, more than any other, so the mode is 39.

A data set can have more than one mode, or none at all if every value is different. The
mode is the only measure of centre that works for categorical data.

2.3 Measures of spread

Two data sets can have the same centre and still be very different. A spread measure says
how far the values tend to lie from the centre.

2.3.1 Range and interquartile range

The range is the largest value minus the smallest. For the pump data it is 47 minus 36,
which is 11 litres per minute. The range is simple but depends entirely on the two most
extreme values.

The quartiles split sorted data into four parts with equal counts. The lower quartile,
written Q1, has a quarter of the values below it, and the upper quartile, written Q3, has
a quarter above it. The interquartile range, or IQR, is Q3 minus Q1, and it measures the
spread of the middle half of the data.

There are several slightly different rules for finding quartiles. In this book Q1 is the
median of the lower half of the data and Q3 is the median of the upper half. For the pump
data the lower half is 36, 37, 38, 38, 39, 39, with median 38, and the upper half is 39,
40, 40, 41, 42, 47, with median 40.5. The IQR is therefore 2.5 litres per minute.

2.3.2 Variance and standard deviation

The variance is the mean of the squared distances from the mean. For a sample, the sum of
squared distances is divided by one less than the number of values, which gives a better
estimate of the spread of the whole population the sample came from.

The standard deviation is the square root of the variance. It is in the same units as the
data, which makes it easier to read than the variance. For the pump data the sample
standard deviation is about 2.8 litres per minute.

To compute a sample standard deviation by hand, first find the mean, then subtract it
from each value and square the result, then add the squares, divide by the number of
values minus one, and finally take the square root.

For data that are roughly bell shaped, about two thirds of the values lie within one
standard deviation of the mean and about 95 percent within two standard deviations.

2.4 The shape of a distribution

A distribution is symmetric when its left and right halves are mirror images, as with
many measurement errors. It is skewed to the right when it has a long tail of high
values, and skewed to the left when the long tail is of low values.

Skew shows itself in the relation between the mean and the median. In a right skewed
distribution the mean is larger than the median, because the tail drags the mean upwards.
The pump data are slightly right skewed: the mean of 39.7 is above the median of 39.

A histogram is the usual way to see the shape. The range of the data is divided into
equal intervals, called bins, and a bar is drawn over each bin with a height equal to the
number of values in it. Too few bins hide the shape; too many make it ragged. Between
five and fifteen bins suits most small data sets.

2.5 Outliers

An outlier is a value that lies far from the rest of the data. It may be a recording
mistake, a fault in the thing measured, or a true but rare value, and the right response
is different in each case.

A common rule marks a value as an outlier when it lies more than 1.5 times the IQR below
Q1 or above Q3. For the pump data, 1.5 times the IQR is 3.75, so the fences are 38 minus
3.75 and 40.5 plus 3.75, that is 34.25 and 44.25. The reading of 47 lies above the upper
fence and is an outlier.

Never delete an outlier only because it is inconvenient. Check where it came from first.
In the pump example the reading of 47 turned out to come from a bench whose flow meter
had not been zeroed, so it was a measuring fault and could be set aside with a note
saying why.

2.6 Correlation

When two numerical variables are measured on the same observations, it is natural to ask
whether they move together. A scatter plot, with one variable on each axis and one point
per observation, is the first thing to draw.

The correlation coefficient, written r, measures how closely the points lie to a straight
line. It runs from minus one to plus one. A value near plus one means that high values of
one variable go with high values of the other; a value near minus one means that high
values go with low values; a value near zero means there is no straight line pattern.

Correlation is not causation. Two variables can be strongly correlated because a third
variable drives both of them. Ice cream sales and sunburn cases rise together, but both
are driven by sunny weather, and neither causes the other.

A correlation coefficient also misses curved patterns. Points that lie on a perfect U
shape can have a correlation near zero. Always look at the scatter plot before trusting
the number.

2.7 Samples and bias

A population is the whole group that a question is about, and a sample is the part of it
that is actually measured. Statistics from a good sample give good estimates of the
population, but only if the sample is chosen well.

A sample is biased when the way it is chosen makes some members of the population more
likely to be included than others in a way linked to what is being measured. Testing only
the pumps that come back for repair, for example, would give a poor picture of the flow
of pumps in general.

The surest guard against bias is random selection, in which every member of the
population has the same chance of being chosen. Larger random samples give estimates that
vary less from one sample to the next, but a large biased sample is still biased.

2.8 Reporting summary statistics

A summary is only useful if the reader can tell what it is. Always state which measure of
centre and which measure of spread are given, the units, and the number of observations
behind them. A line such as "median 39 L/min, IQR 2.5 L/min, twelve pumps" tells the
reader far more than "average about 40".

Round summaries to a sensible number of figures. As a guide, give the mean and standard
deviation to one more decimal place than the raw data. Giving the mean of the pump
readings as 39.666667 suggests a precision that the bench cannot deliver.

When outliers have been set aside, say how many and why. When data are missing, say how
many observations are missing and whether there is any reason to think they differ from
the rest.

A five number summary lists the minimum, Q1, median, Q3 and maximum. For the pump data it
is 36, 38, 39, 40.5 and 47. It is the basis of the box plot, in which a box runs from Q1
to Q3 with a line at the median, and whiskers reach out to the most extreme values that
are not outliers.

Summary
-------

The mean, median and mode describe the centre of a data set; the median resists extreme
values and the mode suits categorical data. The range, interquartile range and standard
deviation describe the spread. The relation between mean and median hints at skew, and a
histogram shows the shape directly. The 1.5 IQR rule flags possible outliers, which
should be checked, not deleted. The correlation coefficient measures straight line
association but says nothing about cause. Good estimates need samples chosen at random.

Exercises
---------

Exercise A. Find the mean, median and mode of the values 4, 8, 6, 5, 8 and 9.

Exercise B. A second batch of twelve pumps gives readings with a mean of 39.5 and a
standard deviation of 0.9. Compare its consistency with the first batch.

Exercise C. Explain why the median repair time is a better summary than the mean repair
time for a workshop report.

Exercise D. Using the 1.5 IQR rule, decide whether a reading of 35 would count as an
outlier in the pump data.

Exercise E. Give an example of two quantities that are correlated because of a third
quantity.
